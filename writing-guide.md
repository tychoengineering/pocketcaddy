# Technical Writing Guide

## Principles of Plain Language

Websites, emails, and reports serve diverse audiences — leadership seeking metrics, product teams researching trends,
analysts documenting metrics, and managers planning roadmaps. Without transparent communication, we cannot access
information effectively. Plain language tackles usability issues with the key goals:

- Identify your specific audience
- Produce understandable written and visual content
- Validate that messaging communicates intended meaning

The fundamental principle: write for your audience. Effective writing matches your readers' existing knowledge,
expertise, and interests. Subject matter experts deserve sophisticated language; non-experts require appropriate
explanations. Recommended best practices:

- Begin with topic sentences that establish structure
- Use active voice for clarity
- Structure information logically
- Use tables for data presentation
- Use lists for conveying multiple details without overwhelming readers

---

## Write for the Reader

### Learn About Your Users

Understanding your audience is foundational. Key techniques:

- **Listen to inquiries**: Monitor email and office contacts to understand common questions
- **Engage directly**: Ask users about their needs and preferences
- **Review analytics**: Examine web metrics including most-visited pages, time spent on pages, and top search phrases

### Focus on What Users Want to Know

People only want to know what applies to them. Identify who your readers are and what matters to them. Contextualize
information with relevance statements like:

- "If you own a KPI, here's how to add it to the dashboard"
- "If you're preparing the quarterly review, start here"

### Address Separate Audiences Separately

Documents serving multiple audiences risk confusing readers. When metrics or recommendations apply differently to
various teams, organize content so each audience can easily locate relevant material. This is especially critical for
reports that several teams act on.

## Organize the Information

### Guide Your Audience Through the Topic

Before writing, consider your audience's existing knowledge and needs. Address these questions:

- Who is my audience?
- What does my audience already know about the subject?
- What does my audience need to know?
- What questions will my audience have?
- What's the best outcome for my team?
- What's the best outcome for our audience?

### Make It Easy to Follow

Strong organization is essential. Start by stating your purpose and the bottom line. Lay things out in a logical order.
Prioritize information strategically:

- Place the most important information at the beginning
- Include background information toward the end

Readers at work seek quick answers about what happened and what to do next. For complex reports, use comprehensive
tables of contents and subheadings to create a reliable navigation structure.

### Process Order

For procedural information, use chronological sequencing. This works well for how-to content, such as a data-access
request:

1. You submit a request for dataset access
2. The data owner reviews the request
3. The owner approves or denies the request
4. You get an email confirming access

### General First, Exceptions Later

Place general information before specialized details. This ensures material relevant to most readers appears first, with
exceptions and conditions following afterward.

## Short and Simple

### Avoid Unnecessary Words

Wordy, dense construction is a significant challenge in workplace writing. Long, complex sentences with multiple phrases
and clauses confuse readers. To address this:

- Critique your own writing
- Ask of every word: "Do you need it?" "Does it provide value?"

Pronouns, active voice, and base verbs help eliminate excess words, as does removing unnecessary modifiers:

- "Sales and Marketing issued a joint report" — the word "joint" is redundant
- "This information is really critical" — "really" adds nothing

### Cut Excess Modifiers

Modifiers like _absolutely_, _actually_, _completely_, _really_, _quite_, _totally_, and _very_ are often unnecessary.

| Say                                             | Instead of                                                                                                       |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Their claim was absurd.                         | Their claim was totally unrealistic.                                                                             |
| ---                                             | ---                                                                                                              |
| It is difficult to reconcile differing views.   | It is particularly difficult to reconcile the somewhat differing views expressed by management.                  |
| Disclosing all facts is important for accuracy. | Total disclosure of all facts is very important to make sure we draw up a total and completely accurate picture. |

### Avoid Doublets and Triplets

Remove repeated concepts expressed through different words.

| Say                             | Instead of                |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Due                             | Due and payable           |
| ---                             | ---                       |
| Stop                            | Cease and desist          |
| Either knowledge or information | Knowledge and information |

### Consistency Counts

Using different terms for the same concept confuses readers. If you reference "active users," maintain that terminology
throughout rather than substituting "engaged users." Reports convey findings and recommendations. While varied word
choice can enhance interest, it compromises clarity.

---

## Avoid Jargon

Jargon is complicated language used to impress, rather than to inform, your audience. When eliminating jargon, the goal
isn't removing necessary technical terms — rather, ensuring language remains as clear as possible. **Example:** Clear:
_Restart the pipeline if it fails._ Jargon-laden: _Initiate a re-execution of the data pipeline process to ensure that
the workflow reaches a successful completion state in the event that a failure condition is encountered._

### Everyday Language

Readers cite jargon as their most frequent writing complaint because writers underestimate how unfamiliar specialized
terms are to their audiences.

| Plain Language      | Jargon                                       |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Weekly active users | Weekly engaged user population               |
| ---                 | ---                                          |
| The report is late. | The report is experiencing a delivery delay. |
| We fixed the bug.   | We implemented a remediation of the defect.  |

When technical language is unavoidable, define terms at the point of use.

### Definition Guidelines

- Place definitions at the document's end, allowing readers to access content first
- List alphabetically rather than numbering for easier navigation
- Avoid defining common words with non-standard meanings
- Don't define unused terms, which confuses readers expecting content on those topics

The fundamental principle: write to communicate, not to impress.

## Writing for Understanding

Plain language writing involves communicating clearly with your specific audience. Research demonstrates that content
becomes easier to understand when incorporating shorter words, brief sections, active voice, and present tense.

## Use the Active Voice

Active voice clarifies who should perform what action, eliminating ambiguity about responsibilities. The subject of an
active sentence performs the action; in passive sentences, the subject receives the action.

| Active Voice                                           | Passive Voice                                         |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| The outage broke the dashboard.                        | The dashboard was broken by the outage.               |
| ---                                                    | ---                                                   |
| We updated the retention metric.                       | The retention metric was updated.                     |
| You must include the following fields in your request. | The following fields must be included in the request. |

Passive constructions typically feature:

- A form of "to be" (are, was, were, could be, have been)
- A past participle (usually ending in "-ed")

Passive voice may occasionally be appropriate when describing actions that follow others as a matter of law without an
identifiable actor.

## Use the Present Tense

Present tense is the simplest and strongest verb form. It makes writing more direct and forceful, requiring less effort
from readers. **Stronger:** "These sections tell you how to meet the requirements." **Weaker:** "These sections describe
types of information that would satisfy requirements."

## Avoid Hidden Verbs

Hidden verbs (nominalizations) convert verbs into nouns, weakening writing and increasing word count.

| Direct                 | Hidden                                            |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| We analyze data.       | We conduct an analysis of data.                   |
| ---                    | ---                                               |
| We manage the program. | We are responsible for management of the program. |

Hidden verbs often feature endings like -ment, -tion, -sion, or -ance, and frequently appear between "the" and "of."

## Clear and Short

### Write Short Sentences

Express only one idea in each sentence. "Long, complicated sentences often mean that you aren't sure about what you want
to say." Shorter sentences facilitate comprehension by breaking information into digestible units. **Example:** **Say:**
Once we establish your goals, we identify one or more potential employers. We prepare a preliminary proposal to present
to an employer who agrees to negotiate a job that meets both his and your employment needs. **Instead of:** Once the
candidate's goals are established, one or more potential employers are identified. A preliminary proposal for
presentation to the employer is developed. The proposal is presented to an employer who agrees to negotiate an
individualized job that meets the employment needs of the applicant and real business needs of the employer.

### Write Short Paragraphs

Short paragraphs are easier to read and understand. Writing experts recommend paragraphs containing no more than 150
words and three to eight sentences. Paragraphs should never exceed 250 words. Vary paragraph lengths for interest.
Single-sentence paragraphs are acceptable occasionally. Short paragraphs create visual breathing room, making content
more inviting and readable.

### Cover One Topic in Each Paragraph

Restrict each paragraph to one topic for improved clarity. Begin with a topic sentence that encapsulates the paragraph's
main idea.

### Write Short Sections

Short sections break up material so it appears easier to comprehend. Dense sections with minimal white space appear
overwhelming and reduce readability. Short sections accommodate useful headings that facilitate skimming and scanning.
Lengthy sections complicate meaningful heading development.

## Familiar Terms

### Use Simple Words and Phrases

Choose commonly understood words over obscure ones.

| Say Instead      | Avoid                  |
| ---------------- | ---------------------- |
| You              | addressee              |
| ---              | ---                    |
| aid, help        | assist, assistance     |
| begin, start     | commence               |
| carry out, start | implement              |
| for, so          | in order that          |
| for              | in the amount of       |
| if               | in the event of        |
| issue, publish   | promulgate             |
| us, we           | this activity, command |
| use              | utilize, utilization   |

### Avoid "Shall"

"Shall" is an aggressive and outdated word often used in legal-style writing. Recommended alternatives:

- Use **must** to express obligations
- Use **must not** for prohibitions
- Use **may** for discretionary actions
- Use **should** for recommendations

Many modern style guides favor "must" over "shall" as part of this shift toward clearer communication.

## Style

### Avoid Noun Strings

Technical and workplace writing frequently overuses noun strings — consecutive nouns clustered together. This reduces
readability, as readers may misidentify which word is the actual noun. **Solutions:**

- Remove non-essential descriptive words
- Use prepositions and articles to clarify word relationships

| Instead of                                         | Write                                                          |
| -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Quarterly user retention report generation process | The process for generating the quarterly user retention report |
| ---                                                | ---                                                            |
| Draft dashboard access request approval guidelines | Draft guidelines for approving dashboard access requests       |

### Check Your Prepositions

Watch for prepositions that often signal phrases reducible to simpler terms.

| Replace                | With                    |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------- |
| a number of            | several, a few, or many |
| ---                    | ---                     |
| a sufficient number of | enough                  |
| at this point in time  | now                     |
| is able to             | can                     |
| on a monthly basis     | monthly                 |
| in order to            | to                      |

### Decrease Abbreviations

#### Use Nicknames

Simplified names for entities improve clarity better than acronyms.

- Instead of "QBR," use: "the quarterly review"
- Instead of "the ETLPS," use: "the pipeline"

#### Common Abbreviations

Well-known abbreviations require no explanation: SQL, API, CSV, URL, PDF, KPI.

#### When Abbreviations Are Necessary

Define abbreviations upon first use: "monthly active users (MAU)." Limit abbreviations per document to two or three
maximum.

### Transition Words

Transitions guide readers between paragraphs and clarify relationships. Three transition types:

- **Pointing words** — Reference previously mentioned items using "this," "that," "these," "those," "the"
- **Echo links** — Repeat or rephrase earlier concepts to maintain connection
- **Explicit connectives** — Supply clear transitions like "further," "also," "however," "therefore"

### Use Examples

Examples clarify complex concepts effectively. Avoid Latin abbreviations "e.g." and "i.e." Write out: "for example" or
"such as."

### Use Positive Language

Positive phrasing improves readability significantly.

| Negative                                             | Positive                                                  |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| If you fail to arrive by 5:00, I cannot pick you up. | You have to arrive by 5:00 if you want me to pick you up. |
| ---                                                  | ---                                                       |

#### Avoid Double Negatives

| Replace              | With            |
| -------------------- | --------------- |
| no fewer than        | at least        |
| ---                  | ---             |
| has not yet attained | is under        |
| may not...until      | may only...when |
| is not...unless      | only if         |

Watch for inherently negative words: unless, fail to, notwithstanding, except, other than.

### Avoid Slashes

Slashes create ambiguity. "And/or" forces readers to determine meaning. Decide whether you mean "and," "or," or "both"
(write "either X, or Y, or both"). Hyphens better join equal terms: "faculty-student ratio."

## Special Cases

### Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

If you write clear web content that is easy to navigate and answers your readers' questions, you will not need to create
a FAQ. FAQs often cause more frustration. Before creating a FAQ, think about:

- What you are trying to say
- What you want people to do with that information
- The most effective way to convey that information

Often, your best solution is a clear, well-organized main content page explaining a process.

#### How to Write Better FAQs

- Supplement your main content with FAQs, but don't try to replace the main content
- Use real customer feedback — track calls and emails to create helpful FAQs
- Use language that non-experts will understand
- Provide unique content — avoid redundant content that can easily become outdated
- Limit the volume of content

#### Designing and Editing FAQs

1. Read through all the FAQs first, then group them by topic or subject
2. Develop headings for each section and organize content in a way that would make sense to the reader
3. Read through each section and edit the content
   - Focus on steps in a process
   - Remove redundancies

### PDFs

Posting PDF versions of original documents on your site may seem like a good alternative to rewriting content in web
format. Unfortunately, PDFs can make it hard for users to understand your content. Many organizations are working to
reduce, remove, and remediate PDFs in favor of web pages. PDF files are:

- Slow to open and can sometimes crash the browser
- Not mobile-friendly
- Difficult for some users to read

If you need to post a PDF, include information about the file on a description page:

- What the PDF is about
- How large the file is
- Who might find the information helpful

### Links

Effective link names are key to satisfying your customers. Eyetracking studies show that links written in plain language
are the most effective.

- Be as explicit as you can — too long is better than too short
- Make the link meaningful — don't use "click here" or "read more"
- Add a short description when needed to clarify the link

Make sure your links are accessible to everyone. Descriptive link text also improves search engine optimization.

## Design for Understanding

Reports and internal pages should help people retrieve information, answer questions, and make decisions with minimal
friction. Well-designed, legible writing surpasses traditional styles in comprehension. Key recommendations:

- Structure pages logically with priority content positioned prominently
- Apply heading levels systematically to establish clear hierarchy
- Craft specific, understandable heading language
- Use lists for quick visual scanning
- Use tables to clarify intricate relationships

### Highlight Important Concepts

Use **bold** and _italics_ to emphasize key details within sections. Avoid underlining, which creates confusion since
web readers associate it with hyperlinks. Caution: PUTTING EVERYTHING IN CAPITAL LETTERS IS NOT A GOOD EMPHASIS
TECHNIQUE — it compromises readability and signals an aggressive tone online. Reserve emphasis for truly significant
information.

---

## Headings

Headings serve as structural elements that guide readers through content logically. Three primary heading types:
**Question headings** — Work best when you understand your audience's needs. Most people come to a report with a
question in mind. Using questions as headings enables readers to quickly locate relevant information. Example: _How does
page design support plain language?_ **Statement headings** — Provide specificity while remaining direct and assertive.
Example: _Design and plain language_ **Topic headings** — Common in reports, but can be vague. Terms like "General" or
"Misc" may confuse readers. Example: _Overview_

### Conciseness and Descriptiveness

Headings should not overwhelm the material. Keep headings shorter than their accompanying content, avoiding single-word
answers when possible.

### Limiting Hierarchy Levels

Keep structural depth to three levels maximum. Most style guides recommend this, as more than three levels make
documents hard to read and use.

---

## Lists

Lists display a series of steps, requirements, or information in a visually clear format. They help readers:

- Emphasize levels of importance
- Grasp the sequence of events
- Enable easy scanning and skimming
- Clarify all process steps
- Provide white space for readability
- Present items, conditions, and exceptions effectively

### Outline Steps in a Process

Number list items when presenting chronological procedures. When a teammate requests a new dashboard, follow these four
steps:

1. Confirm the metrics and date range they need
2. Build the dashboard from the approved data source
3. Share the link and ask for feedback
4. Add the dashboard to the team catalog

### Focus on Readability

Avoid overusing lists — reserve them for highlighting important information. Formatting guidelines:

- Use solid round or square bullets
- Avoid decorative bullet shapes
- Include introductory sentences before each list
- Use left justification only

**Parallel construction matters.** Ensure all list items follow the same grammatical structure and purpose.

---

## Tables

Complex material can be simplified and made clearer through the strategic use of tables. These tools enable audiences to
grasp relationships that might otherwise remain obscured within lengthy paragraphs. Most readers only need to comprehend
the specific conditions relevant to their circumstances, rather than every possible scenario. **Example — retention by
signup cohort:**

| Signup cohort | Week 1 | Week 4 | Week 8 | Week 12 |
| ------------- | ------ | ------ | ------ | ------- |
| January       | 100%   | 62%    | 48%    | 41%     |
| ---           | ---    | ---    | ---    | ---     |
| February      | 100%   | 65%    | 51%    | 44%     |
| March         | 100%   | 59%    | 45%    | 39%     |
| April         | 100%   | 68%    | 55%    | 47%     |

Tables require specific accessibility standards: label header rows, avoid merged cells, and give the table a caption.

---

## Visuals

The selective use of images and video can help increase understanding. Visuals are especially effective for
communicating complex ideas — processes, relationships, or spatial information — that are difficult to convey in text
alone. Consider using visuals when:

- A concept involves multiple interacting elements (e.g., how a request flows through several services)
- A step-by-step process benefits from a diagram
- Readers need to compare or recognize something visually

---

## Links

Thoughtful use of links can help readers find key information.

### Avoid Overuse

Links and cross-references can disrupt any attempt to write clearly and simply. Excessive cross-references may confuse
users and strain their short-term memory. Exercise restraint rather than overwhelming users with too many links.

### Provide Descriptive Links

Link text should be descriptive and contain all the information the user needs to decide whether to click.

| Say                                                                                                     | Instead of                                                           |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| You may wish to review this _introduction to human-centered design_ to learn more about HCD principles. | You may wish to review this _introduction_ to human-centered design. |
| ---                                                                                                     | ---                                                                  |

### Accessibility Best Practices for Links to PDFs

Include file information within the linked text:

- Whether they're opening a file or web page
- Document type (in case software installation is needed)
- File size (relevant for limited bandwidth or storage)
- Publication date (especially for regularly updated documents)

**Example:** _Q3 Metrics Review (PDF, 220 KB, 32 pages)_

## Test for Understanding

Content testing should be integrated throughout your writing and planning process rather than as an afterthought. This
is particularly vital for reports that reach many teams and drive decisions. Testing your writing to make sure it's
clear to users can save you time in answering questions later. Testing applies across various formats including
websites, brochures, videos, social media, and public affairs materials.

### When to Test

Begin testing as soon as sufficient material exists — don't delay until coding is complete or documents are finalized.
Early testing enables iterative improvement. Test at minimum twice.

### Recommended Testing Techniques

Three primary approaches:

- **Paraphrase Testing** — Individual interviews; best for short web pages, brief documents, and survey questions
- **Usability Testing** — Individual interviews; ideal for longer documents and websites where finding information
  matters
- **Controlled Comparative Studies** — Large-scale studies collecting statistical response data; implement smaller-scale
  paraphrase and usability testing first

### Focus Groups

Focus groups are discussions in which you learn about users' attitudes and expectations more than about whether they can
find and understand information. They're more valuable for understanding audiences before writing than for testing
finished content.

---

## Paraphrase Testing

Paraphrase testing evaluates whether readers accurately comprehend your intended message. It works best for short
documents, web pages, and survey questions. With just 6 to 9 participants, this approach requires minimal time
investment.

### How to Conduct Paraphrase Testing

Conduct 6 to 9 one-on-one interviews. Have participants read specific sections and explain what each section means in
their own words. Record their explanations without correcting them. Misunderstandings reveal content problems requiring
revision. **Follow-up questions:**

- What actions would you take after reading this?
- What was the writer's purpose?
- How might this content work for others you know?

The final question is valuable because people often more readily identify confusion in hypothetical readers rather than
themselves. Avoid yes/no questions — they yield minimal usable information.

### Real-World Examples

**Ambiguous timeframes:** A note saying "The dashboard will be updated shortly" prompted readers to ask when. Giving an
approximate date eliminated the follow-up questions. **Unclear terminology:** Readers interpreted "active user"
differently — some counted any login, others only weekly use. Defining the term ensured uniform understanding. **Loaded
language:** Adding "qualified" to "leads" created three distinct interpretations among readers, reducing data accuracy.

---

## Usability Testing

Usability testing is particularly valuable "when people have to find your information in order to understand it." This
method examines content holistically rather than isolating individual sections.

### When to Conduct Testing

Testing can occur at any point you have draft material. Additional rounds can validate whether revisions addressed
identified issues without creating new ones.

### Participant Selection

Recruit 3 to 5 individuals matching your target audience criteria. No special permissions are required for small-scale
testing with this participant count. Allocate one hour per volunteer.

### Session Structure

**Introduction**: Establish comfort, explain procedures, and gather background information about participants.
**Scenarios**: Present brief narratives requiring specific information retrieval. Participants "think aloud" while
searching, revealing their language and comprehension. **Debriefing**: Ask neutral follow-up questions about experience
and specific terminology.

### Testing Variations

- Co-discovery (two participants collaborating)
- Group sessions with multiple simultaneous participants
- Comparative versions of documents
- Remote moderated testing
- Remote unmoderated testing (tools can record sessions for later review)

### Case Study: Retitling a Report

Testing revealed that "Power Users Churn, Too" confused readers about the intended audience. Retitling to "Why Any User
Churns" and subsequent retesting demonstrated improved audience comprehension.

---

## Comparative Studies

Controlled comparative studies (A/B testing or split testing) give you quantitative data on how well your audience uses
your content. Before doing a controlled comparative study, do paraphrase testing or usability testing and refine your
writing based on what you learn. Controlled comparative studies are best near the end of the process — they tell you
_if_ your content is working, but not _why_.

### Define Your Goals

Before you do a controlled study, know what results you will consider a success:

- Do you want more people to open and read a report?
- Do you want fewer follow-up questions asking for clarification?
- Do you want more people to act on a recommendation?
- Do you want fewer errors when people use a dashboard?

### Testing Websites

Set your web server to send each variation on a specific schedule. Track whatever measure you want by which version the
web visitor saw.

### Testing Documents

Send a small test group the new version of your document and a control group the old document. Track the responses to
all documents and compare the results. It is much easier to test results when people return a written response than when
you try to track the number of questions you receive. If you have an analyst on hand, they can tell you how many people
you need to make your study statistically valid. You can record what percentage of your "before" version generates
correct responses compared to your "after" version, or what percentage of each version results in a reader asking you
for an explanation.

## ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English

Simplified Technical English (STE) is a controlled-language standard (ASD-STE100) built for technical documentation —
procedures, maintenance instructions, and reference material read by a global audience, including non-native English
speakers. It reduces ambiguity so one sentence has exactly one meaning. STE has two parts:

- **Writing rules** — grammar, style, and structure (below)
- **Dictionary** — a controlled vocabulary that pairs approved words with unapproved alternatives

The core principle: **one word, one meaning; one meaning, one word.** Use each approved word in a single sense, and use
a single word for each concept.

## Words

- Use only approved words from the STE dictionary in their approved meaning and part of speech.
- Use approved technical names (nouns) and technical verbs freely — hardware, tools, and processes keep their real
  names.
- Use one word for one concept. Don't switch between synonyms (`start`, not `start`/`begin`/`initiate`).
- Don't use a word as more than one part of speech. `oil` the noun and `oil` the verb conflict — write "apply oil."

| Approved | Not approved        |
| -------- | ------------------- |
| start    | commence, initiate  |
| ---      | ---                 |
| do, make | accomplish, perform |
| about    | approximately       |
| follow   | adhere to           |
| let      | permit, allow       |

## Noun phrases

- Keep noun clusters to three words maximum. Break longer strings with prepositions and articles.
- Always use articles (`a`, `an`, `the`) and other determiners — don't drop them for brevity.

| Instead of                         | Write                                         |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| runway light connection resistance | the resistance of the runway light connection |
| ---                                | ---                                           |
| aircraft position error report     | a report of the aircraft position error       |

## Verbs

- Use only the approved verb tenses: the simple present, simple past, and simple future.
- Form the past with the past participle only in the passive of an approved verb; avoid perfect and progressive tenses.
- Don't use the `-ing` form (gerunds and participles) unless it's part of an approved technical name.
- Use the active voice in procedures. Use the passive only when the actor is unknown or irrelevant, or in descriptive
  writing where the passive is clearer.

| Instead of                                     | Write                             |
| ---------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| The bearings are being lubricated by the pump. | The pump lubricates the bearings. |
| ---                                            | ---                               |
| You will have completed the test.              | You complete the test.            |

## Sentences

- Keep procedural sentences to **20 words** maximum; descriptive sentences to **25 words** maximum.
- Express one instruction per sentence. Two actions means two sentences (unless they happen at the same time).
- Start an instruction with a command verb: "Open the valve," not "The valve should be opened."
- Use vertical lists to break complex conditions or actions apart.
- Repeat a key word rather than replace it with a synonym or pronoun that could be ambiguous.

## Paragraphs

- Keep each paragraph to one topic. Start with the topic sentence.
- Limit a paragraph to **six sentences** maximum.

## Procedures

- Write each step as a separate, numbered instruction in chronological order.
- Use the imperative: the reader is the subject, so start with the verb.
- Put a conditional or location phrase first, then the command: "Before you start the engine, remove the cover."

To replace the filter:

1. Set the switch to OFF.
2. Remove the four bolts.
3. Remove the old filter.
4. Install the new filter.

## Descriptive writing

- Use the present tense to describe how something works.
- Vary sentence length for readability, but stay within the 25-word limit.
- Connect related sentences with approved connectives (`but`, `then`, `because`).

## Warnings and cautions

- Put the warning or caution **before** the step it applies to.
- Start with a clear command that says what to do or not do, then explain the reason.

**Warning:** Do not touch the connector. High voltage can cause injury.

## Punctuation and word count

- Use simple punctuation. Avoid slashes (`/`); write "either X or Y."
- Don't use parentheses for essential information — put it in a sentence.
- Spell out or define abbreviations at first use, and keep them to a minimum.
